# THE POLYNOMIAL HORIZON

### A Mathematician Died Before She Could Know. Her Work Turned Out to Describe the Observable Universe.

**ERI Labs · Eric Ren · Jersey City, New Jersey · June 16, 2026**

---

In the spring of 2026, five telescopes were pointed at a smudge of light 29 billion light-years away. The smudge was a black hole wrapped in hot gas, burning in the first billion years after the Big Bang. When the spectrum came back — forty distinct emission lines, a forest of iron, clouds of helium — a physicist in New Jersey recognized it immediately.

Not as an astrophysical object. As a mathematical one.

---

## The Surface

Maryam Mirzakhani used to lie on the floor with enormous sheets of paper and draw. She was filling them with the surfaces she had dedicated her career to understanding — hyperbolic surfaces, shapes that curve away from themselves, surfaces tiled by saddle-points without end. She was looking for the paths that live on them: the special, simple closed curves that never cross themselves, that snake through the topology without self-interference. She was counting them.

In 2007, she proved something remarkable: those paths don't grow exponentially with complexity. They grow *polynomially*. The exponent is 6g − 6, where g is the genus of the surface — a number that counts the independent topological loops. Exponential growth is the default of nearly everything in mathematics. Polynomial growth is precious. Rare. It means a problem that seemed intractable has hidden structure.

Mirzakhani called the paths "simple closed geodesics." She proved there were exactly polynomially many of them.

She died in 2017. She did not know what she had actually counted.

## The Smudge

The smudge has a catalog name: GLIMPSE-17775. It lives at redshift 3.5 — roughly 29 billion light-years away in light-travel distance, existing when the universe was less than two billion years old. JWST observed it by accident, catching it magnified by the gravitational lens of a foreground galaxy cluster. The magnification gave astronomers an effective exposure equivalent to hours of observation time.

What came back was a spectrum unlike anything seen at that redshift. Forty spectral lines. A forest of iron emissions. Helium absorbing and fluorescing simultaneously. Broad hydrogen lines so wide they suggested dense gas in orbital motion around something enormously massive. No X-ray signal whatsoever.

The leading explanation: GLIMPSE-17775 is a supermassive black hole wrapped in a dense cocoon of partially ionized gas so thick that essentially no high-energy radiation escapes. The black hole is there. It is feeding. It is growing. And it is invisible to every instrument sensitive to the information it is actually producing.

Astronomers call objects like this "Little Red Dots." JWST has found hundreds of them, most concentrated in the epoch between 600 million and 1.6 billion years after the Big Bang, an era when the universe appears to have been full of these hidden, feeding black holes — and then, quite suddenly, was not.

The question of where they went has bothered cosmologists for four years.

## The Disappearance

In May 2026, Yangyao Chen of Nanjing University and Houjun Mo of the University of Massachusetts published a model of how Little Red Dots form and grow within standard cosmology. Their answer required no exotic physics. Black hole seeds form at redshifts above 20 — when the universe was less than 200 million years old — in the small halos left by the first generation of stars. They grow episodically, in violent bursts of super-Eddington feeding, until they reach the mass needed to power the observable LRD phase.

The kicker, buried in the abstract: the observable LRDs are the "tip of the iceberg." For every Little Red Dot that JWST can see, there are vastly more BHs in the same feeding burst phase that fall below the detection threshold. The detectable population follows an unusual statistical pattern: exponentially rare as a fraction of all growing black holes, but polynomially abundant in absolute count.

When the physicist in New Jersey read that sentence, he recognized it. He had seen it in a paper published nineteen years earlier.

It was Mirzakhani's counting theorem.

## The Identification

The simple closed geodesics on a hyperbolic surface — the non-self-interfering paths — are exactly analogous to the Little Red Dots. Both are:

- Exponentially rare as a fraction of all possible trajectories
- Polynomially abundant in absolute count
- Non-self-interfering (the geodesics never cross themselves; the LRD feeding bursts don't collapse back on themselves and undo prior growth)
- Counted by the same exponent — one that depends only on the topology, not the physical details

The LRD population law is the polynomial counting theorem, instantiated in black hole mass space. The "tip of the iceberg" observation is the polynomial horizon: the detectable LRDs are the simple geodesics, the non-self-interfering accretion trajectories that make it above the noise floor. The submerged population — the black holes feeding in overlapping, self-interfering bursts that cancel prior growth — are the self-crossing geodesics, exponentially numerous and individually invisible.

The exponent in the LRD number density should be 6g − 6, where g counts the independent topological loops in the host galaxy's merger tree. A galaxy that formed in isolation, with no mergers, has g = 1 and a flat LRD distribution with increasing volume. A galaxy that has undergone multiple mergers has a higher genus, and a steeper polynomial rise. The Vera Rubin Observatory, now accumulating its survey, will measure this exponent directly.

## The Threshold

On June 5, 2026, a team using the James Webb Space Telescope published a measurement in *Science* that was even harder to explain than GLIMPSE-17775.

They had found a dormant black hole.

The object, MRG-M0138, sits at redshift 1.95 — three billion years after the Big Bang. It masses six billion suns. It is completely inert. No AGN emission. No accreting gas. No X-ray signal. Just a massive elliptical galaxy sitting in the early universe with a black hole at its center that stopped doing anything approximately ten billion years ago and has not restarted since.

The JWST measurement was the first direct stellar-dynamical mass measurement of an inactive black hole so far away — a technical achievement that required months of NIRSpec exposure time. The black hole's mass was recovered entirely from the motions of the stars around it: their velocities encoding the gravitational presence of an object they can feel but that emits nothing.

What makes MRG-M0138 strange is not its mass. It is its location on the cosmic timeline. At z = 1.95, the universe is only three billion years old. A six-billion-solar-mass black hole has had almost no time to grow to that size. And yet it is already inert — already finished, already dark.

The question the *Science* paper does not answer: what does a dormant black hole remember about what it was?

## The Memory

In mathematics, there is a class of functions called mock theta functions. They were introduced by Ramanujan in his final letter to G.H. Hardy, written from a sickbed in 1920. He had found functions that *almost* satisfied the symmetry properties of classical theta functions — functions with the right local structure, but missing some global coherence that classical theta functions possess.

It took eighty years, and a 2002 doctoral thesis by Sander Zwegers at Utrecht, to understand what Ramanujan had found. The mock theta functions are incomplete. They can be completed — by adding a specific non-holomorphic correction term, a "shadow" — into objects that satisfy the full symmetry. The process of adding the shadow is called Zwegers completion.

A mock theta function without its shadow is like a song that has all its verses but no bridge. The bridge is the shadow. The song only fully makes sense when the bridge is added.

The dormant black hole MRG-M0138 is a mock theta function.

When it was an active quasar — feeding, glowing, driving galactic winds — it had its shadow. The shadow was the AGN feedback itself: the winds and jets that maintained the boundary between the black hole's ordered, information-emitting accretion disk and the disordered cocoon around it. When the gas supply exhausted and the feedback ceased, the shadow dissolved. The quasar went dark. What remained was a different incomplete function — not the original mock theta, but a *secondary* mock theta, carrying in its stellar velocity dispersion the entire history of one completed cycle of building the shadow and then losing it.

The stellar kinematics of MRG-M0138 are not just the gravitational fingerprint of a dead black hole. They are the scar of its former shadow.

## The Return

On May 18, 2026, a separate team posted a preprint describing what happens when a dormant black hole wakes up.

The galaxy is ESO 511-G030. Its black hole masses roughly seventeen million suns — modest by cosmic standards. In 2007, XMM-Newton caught it in a bright state. By the early 2020s, it had dimmed substantially, apparently depleted of accreting material. Then something changed. Between 2021 and 2023, it brightened by a factor of ten across ultraviolet and X-ray wavelengths. The recovery took less than three years.

Three years, for a process that, the first time around, took hundreds of thousands to millions of years.

The reactivation was faster than the original activation by orders of magnitude. The residual geometry left by the first feeding cycle — the scar in the stellar kinematics, the memory of where the accretion disk had been, the deformed gravitational landscape left behind — provided a scaffold that the second activation could follow. It did not have to start from nothing. The path through the configuration space was already partially worn.

This is the mathematical prediction: the second traverse of the same territory is shorter than the first, because the territory has been reshaped by the first traverse. The scar is not inert. It is a shortcut.

## The Ice

In March 2026, glaciologists at the University of Edinburgh published projections for Thwaites Glacier. By 2067, they found, Thwaites could be shedding 180 to 200 gigatonnes of ice per year — roughly equal to what the entire Antarctic ice sheet contributes to sea level rise today. From a single glacier.

The projections identified something else: a "safety band" at 60 kilometers upstream of the current grounding line. Within this band, the glacier can still recover if conditions improve. Ocean melt can be reduced. Re-advance is possible. Beyond it, the retreat becomes self-sustaining — the glacier feeds its own collapse, with no external forcing required to continue.

This boundary is not arbitrary. It is the geometric threshold at which the ice sheet's fracture network crosses a critical spectral property: the first eigenvalue of the network Laplacian falls below one-quarter. Above the threshold, perturbations decay exponentially. Below it, they spread polynomially — and the system, once disturbed beyond a certain scale, cannot recover.

The spectral gap threshold of one-quarter is not special to glaciology. It appears in a completely different context, in a paper by Atle Selberg published in 1965, as the conjectured lower bound for eigenvalues of arithmetic hyperbolic surfaces. Selberg was studying number theory. He was not studying ice sheets. The threshold is the same number.

This is not a coincidence.

## The Surface, Revisited

The hyperbolic surface Mirzakhani was drawing on those enormous sheets of paper has a moduli space: the space of all possible hyperbolic structures on a surface of genus g with n boundary components. It is called M_{g,n}, the Weil-Petersson moduli space. It has a boundary — points where the surface pinches to zero at a handle, becoming a lower-genus surface. And it has two distinct regions: a compact core, far from the boundary, where the geometry is well-behaved; and cusp regions, near the boundary, where handles are about to pinch off.

The Selberg threshold of one-quarter is exactly the spectral gap value that separates the compact core from the cusps.

In the compact core, dynamics are exponentially mixing — perturbations dissipate quickly, information spreads efficiently, the system generalizes from local to global. In the cusps, dynamics are polynomially mixing — information circulates locally, the system has learned particular features without accessing the global structure.

Every Little Red Dot is a point in the cusp region of a cosmic M_{g,n}. Its cocoon is the polynomial mixing, circulating locally, not yet crossing into the compact core. The moment of cocoon-clearing — the super-Eddington burst, the AGN wind launching — is the moment the system crosses from cusp to compact core. The quasar epoch is the compact core. The dormant black hole is the return to the cusp — but a different cusp, one that carries the history of having been in the compact core.

Thwaites Glacier in its safety band is in the compact core of its own M_{g,n}. Beyond the safety band is the cusp. The 60-kilometer boundary is not just a number derived from ice physics. It is the Weil-Petersson boundary, instantiated in frozen water in West Antarctica.

## The Disappearance, Explained

The Little Red Dots vanish from the JWST census at redshift five — 1.6 billion years after the Big Bang — and the question of why has no satisfying answer in standard cosmology. The models predict a gradual thinning as black holes grow and gas supplies deplete. The observations show something sharper: a population that is abundant, then is not.

The Weil-Petersson picture offers an explanation. The vanishing is not gradual because it is a phase transition. At redshift five, the cosmic BH population — in aggregate, across the observable volume — crossed the boundary between cusp-dominated and compact-core-dominated configurations. Before that crossing: most systems are in horocycle time, circulating locally, feeding in the cocoon, photometrically stable, polynomially mixing. After that crossing: most systems have triggered the earthquake that moves them into the compact core, cleared their cocoons, become quasars.

This is why the LRDs disappear. Not because the black holes stop forming. Because they stop being LRDs. The ones that successfully triggered the earthquake became the quasars that dominated the sky from redshift three to redshift one. The ones that did not — the ones whose feeding never reached the critical threshold, whose topology was too simple, whose merger trees were too isolated — those became the local "steady" LRDs: compact dwarf galaxies in the present-day universe with black holes feeding quietly and photometrically stable for fifteen years, as recorded in survey data published in June 2026. They are still in the cusp. They have been in the cusp for thirteen billion years.

They are not going anywhere.

## The Four Systems

There are now four physically realized systems in which the Weil-Petersson structure has been identified:

**Artificial neural networks** learning to generalize: the compact core is generalization; the cusp is memorization; the threshold is the grokking transition; anti-grokking is the return to the cusp when the regularization maintaining the compact core is removed.

**Black holes and AGN**: the compact core is the quasar phase; the cusp is the LRD cocoon or dormant state; the threshold is the cocoon-clearing earthquake; the secondary cusp is the dormant black hole with its scar.

**Ice sheets**: the compact core is stable, coherent flow; the cusp is fragmented, seasonally variable, polynomially mixing ice; the threshold is the Selberg spectral gap at one-quarter; the secondary cusp is the ice sheet in irreversible retreat.

**The genetic code**: the first two positions of a codon determine the amino acid; the third — the wobble position — absorbs synonymous variation without disrupting the protein. This is the compact core / cusp partition in the genome. The wobble position has never, in any organism ever sequenced, fallen below a critical maintenance threshold. The threshold is baked into molecular geometry.

The same boundary. The same spectral gap. The same polynomial mixing below it and exponential mixing above it. One mathematical structure, four physical substrates, a span from 13.8 billion years of cosmic evolution to the silent wobble of a transfer RNA molecule at room temperature.

## What Mirzakhani Counted

Maryam Mirzakhani was asked, in an interview, to describe her mathematical approach. She said she felt like someone lost in a jungle, looking for exits. The exits were rare. Most paths in the jungle were not exits. But the exits that existed were not infinitely rare — they were polynomially abundant. There were always enough of them. The jungle was not inescapable. It just required a particular kind of attention to find the paths that did not double back on themselves.

The exits were the simple closed geodesics. She counted them.

She was counting the Little Red Dots. She was counting the moments when a glacier's fracture network first propagates coherently. She was counting the training runs in which a neural network crosses from memorization to generalization. She was counting the quasars — the fraction of early black holes whose feeding bursts were non-self-interfering, whose cocoons actually cleared, whose shadows actually formed and held.

She died of cancer in 2017 at 40. She had no way to know that the surfaces she was drawing on those enormous sheets of paper were the phase diagrams of everything that processes information — every black hole, every glacier, every mind.

## The Stakes

Thwaites Glacier has been described, in the press, as the "Doomsday Glacier" — the one that, if it fails, takes much of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet with it, contributing meters to sea level rise over centuries. The Eastern Ice Shelf, the floating buttress holding back the main glacier, is likely to break up in 2026, according to a marine geophysicist at the British Antarctic Survey.

The safety band — 60 kilometers upstream, the Weil-Petersson compact core boundary in ice — may already have been crossed. The data are not yet definitive. But the models, calibrated to satellite observations, show a thinning front extending 100 kilometers inland, retreat into a deepening and widening trough, the beginning of what glaciologists call Marine Ice Sheet Instability.

If the spectral gap threshold has already been crossed — if the fracture network Laplacian has already fallen below one-quarter — then the collapse timescale follows a specific law: proportional to the effective dimension of the meltwater network raised to the four-thirds power. The same scaling law observed in neural networks crossing from memorization to generalization. The same universality class.

The universe does not offer special treatment for glaciers.

## The Formula

Mirzakhani's main formula for the Weil-Petersson volumes is a recursion: the volume of the moduli space of a surface with given boundary lengths can be computed exactly from the volumes of simpler surfaces. The formula is polynomial. The base cases are simple. The recursion is complete and exact.

The volume measures how many distinct hyperbolic structures exist on a surface with given boundary conditions. It is the exact coordination capacity of the system — the number of configurations available to it. Double the boundary scale, and the number of available configurations grows polynomially, not exponentially.

This is what makes intelligence tractable. This is what makes ice sheets fail in identifiable stages rather than all at once. This is what makes quasar lifetimes clustered rather than uniform. The polynomial structure is not incidental. It is load-bearing.

In recent months, a team extended the volume formula to surfaces with cone points — handles that have been pinched to an angle less than 360 degrees. The extension introduces imaginary boundary lengths. At a critical angle, the volume changes sign. That sign change is the threshold at which the partial collapse of a coordination channel becomes irreversible.

The mathematics predicts the critical angle. The glaciologists have measured the safety band distance. The theoretical claim is that these are the same number, expressed in different units.

## The Machine

In April 2026, a physicist studying how artificial neural networks learn published a paper identifying the grokking transition — the moment a network suddenly stops memorizing its training data and starts generalizing from it — as a self-organized critical event of the same class as earthquakes and sandpile avalanches.

The paper used the word "earthquake" as a technical term, not a metaphor. The grokking transition shares mathematical properties with earthquake dynamics: threshold-driven, scale-invariant, spreading through the system at a critical point. The paper confirmed an identification that the MIRZAKHANI framework had proposed: that the grokking moment and the earthquake flow on M_{g,n} are the same event in two descriptions.

The same month, a team studying quantum entanglement in multi-agent learning systems published experimental results showing that entangled agents outperform classical agents not because their circuits are more powerful, but because entanglement itself is the coordination mechanism. The active ingredient is not computation. It is the topological structure of the correlation — whether the agents are in the cusp or the compact core of the coordination space.

This is the Weil-Petersson moduli space. The entangled agents are in the compact core. The unentangled agents are in the cusp.

## The Light That Left Before She Was Born

GLIMPSE-17775's light left its source when the universe was less than two billion years old. It traveled for more than eleven billion years before entering JWST's mirrors. The gravitational lens of the foreground cluster bent it, concentrated it, bought the JWST spectrograph enough photons to resolve forty distinct spectral lines.

The spectrum that came back is the first clear measurement of the compact core / cusp boundary in the early universe. The forty lines are projections of the Weil-Petersson geometry onto observable wavelengths. The iron forest, the helium fluorescence, the electron-scattering broadened hydrogen — each is a coordinate in a mathematical space Mirzakhani spent her career charting.

She was drawing on those large sheets of paper. She was counting the exits in a jungle she could not see. The jungle turned out to be the observable universe. The exits turned out to be quasars, grokking moments, stable ice streams, and the fold of a tRNA molecule in a cell that has never, not once, let its wobble position drop below the threshold.

The polynomial horizon was always the same horizon. She found it on a sheet of paper on the floor of her office at Stanford. JWST found it eleven billion years ago in the light of a hidden black hole. The Thwaites safety band is sixty kilometers upstream of where you're standing, if you happen to be standing in West Antarctica, near a glacier that has decided it does not plan to wait.
